Learning to Live With Anxiety on the Trail

July 5, 2026

Summary

One question I’m occasionally asked is whether I still feel anxious when documenting trails alone.

The honest answer is yes.

After documenting many trails across Cebu, I’ve realized that anxiety has never completely disappeared. What has changed is my understanding of what usually causes it. In my experience, the feeling is less about the trail itself and more about uncertainty. Dense forests, poor visibility, changing weather, unfamiliar surroundings, and disappearing mobile signal can all contribute to that uncertainty, even when a trail is not especially hazardous.

This journal records personal observations made during beesayatv field visits. It reflects my own experiences and should not be interpreted as medical, psychological, or professional advice.

A Pattern I Didn’t Expect

When I first started documenting trails, I assumed that experience would eventually remove the feeling of anxiety.

It didn’t.

What changed instead was how I interpreted it.

Looking back through my field notes, I noticed that the same types of situations appeared repeatedly. Dense vegetation reduced visibility. Multiple side trails made navigation less certain. Dark clouds changed the mood of a hike. River crossings increased the commitment to continue. Losing mobile signal often made a place feel more isolated than it actually was.

Individually, none of these observations necessarily meant there was danger ahead. Together, however, they often created a greater sense of uncertainty.

That has been one of the most consistent patterns I’ve observed while documenting trails.

Camp 8 to Matun-og Falls

One field visit that clearly illustrates this happened while documenting the route between Camp 8 and Matun-og Falls.

The destination wasn’t far away, but several conditions came together at the same time. Dark clouds were beginning to build, rain appeared to be approaching, I had already crossed the river, and the forest became noticeably denser. The route also included several side trails leading toward nearby farms, which required more attention to navigation.

I knew there were communities in the area, but the trail still felt increasingly isolated because visibility was limited and mobile signal had become unreliable.

After stopping for a few minutes, I decided to turn back.

At the time, I felt slightly disappointed because the documentation wasn’t complete. Looking back now, I see that visit differently. It documented the conditions that existed on that particular day, including the decision to end the field visit early. When I eventually returned and completed the route, both visits became part of the trail’s documented history.

Observations From Other Field Visits

As I continued documenting more trails, I began noticing other recurring patterns.

Trails that initially felt intimidating often became much more familiar after several visits. I started recognizing junctions, remembering landmarks, and understanding how different paths connected to nearby communities. The routes themselves hadn’t necessarily changed, but my familiarity with them had.

I’ve also noticed that anxiety tends to appear gradually rather than all at once. It often begins as a small feeling that something is different from what I expected. Sometimes it’s the weather. Sometimes it’s the environment. Sometimes it’s simply realizing that I’m paying more attention to every sound around me.

Those moments have become part of the documentation as much as the GPX track or the photographs.

Personal Field Notes

Looking back through different field visits, I noticed that I often responded in similar ways whenever uncertainty increased.

Sometimes I talked to myself while walking because it helped me stay mentally focused. On other occasions, I concentrated only on returning to the last place where I felt comfortable instead of thinking about the entire route ahead. I’ve also found myself listening to familiar audio, using a small portable fan during hot weather, splashing cold water on my face, or deliberately ignoring my smartwatch when I knew repeatedly checking my heart rate would only make me more aware of my anxiety.

I’m not presenting these as techniques for others to follow. They’re simply observations about my own behavior that became apparent after documenting many solo field visits.

What I Learned

One lesson I’ve come to appreciate is that documenting trails isn’t only about recording distances, elevation, or navigation.

It also means documenting the decisions made during the visit.

Not every field visit reaches its intended destination, and I’ve stopped viewing that as failure. If changing weather, increasing uncertainty, or other conditions lead me to end the documentation early, then that decision becomes part of the record. It accurately reflects what happened during that visit.

Several trails that I didn’t complete on my first attempt were successfully documented on later visits under different conditions. Looking back, I value both visits equally because each recorded a different moment in the life of the trail.

Closing Thoughts

One of the unexpected things this project has taught me is that uncertainty is often a larger part of solo field documentation than difficulty itself.

Some trails are physically demanding but mentally straightforward. Others are relatively easy to walk yet feel more challenging because the surroundings create a greater sense of uncertainty.

Those experiences have become part of my field journal because they help explain not only the trail I was documenting, but also the circumstances under which it was documented.
